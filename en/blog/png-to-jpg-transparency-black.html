PNG to JPG: Why Transparency Turns Black (And How to Fix It)

Why transparent areas turn black or noisy when converting PNG to JPEG

2026-09-17 · ~950 words · 4 min read

Almost everyone hits the same surprise the first time they convert a transparent PNG to JPEG: the transparent areas turn black (or white, grey, or noisy colour). Your image isn't broken and the tool isn't "broken" — this is a structural property of the format. Here's why it happens and three ways to solve it.

1. The root cause: JPEG has no alpha channel

How does transparency work? In PNG and WebP, each pixel stores not just red, green and blue but also an alpha value (0 = fully transparent, 255 = fully opaque).

JPEG has no alpha channel at all. It stores RGB only. So when you convert PNG to JPEG, there is nowhere to put that alpha information — a decision must be made: what colour should those "transparent" pixels actually be?

Why black is the usual wrong answer

If a converter simply drops the alpha channel without compositing, transparent regions commonly come out black — because in many internal representations a fully transparent pixel carries RGB (0,0,0).

Some tools partially handle it instead, producing grey or coloured noise — that's the semi-transparent anti-aliased edge pixels being written incorrectly.

2. The correct fix: composite onto a background

The standard technique is compositing: fill the canvas with an opaque colour first, then draw the transparent image on top. Transparent areas then show the background, and edges blend correctly.

  1. Create the canvas and fill it with the target colour (white, typically)
  2. Draw the PNG over it (an overlay, not a copy)
  3. Export as JPEG

Result: transparent areas become white, soft edges blend properly, and there is no black blocking or noise.

Option 2 — Composite manually in an image editor

In Photoshop / GIMP / Affinity:

  1. Add a new layer at the very bottom
  2. Fill it with the colour you want (usually white)
  3. Flatten the image
  4. Export as JPEG

Good when you need precise control, but slow for more than a couple of files.

Option 3 — Don't convert to JPEG at all

Often the real requirement is "smaller file", not "specifically JPEG".

If the target supports WebP, converting to WebP is the better move: it keeps transparency and is 25–35% smaller than JPEG. You get both properties at once.

You only need to wrestle with compositing when JPEG is a hard requirement (legacy systems, specific upload forms).

4. A common misunderstanding

"So if I convert JPG to PNG, do I get transparency?"

No. There was never any alpha information in the JPEG to recover. Converting just swaps in a container that can hold alpha — the pixels remain opaque. To get transparency you must cut out the subject in an editor and generate an alpha channel.

5. Pre-flight checklist

  • Does the target genuinely require JPEG? If WebP works, prefer WebP
  • Does the image actually have transparent areas? (Look for the checkerboard)
  • Which background colour? White is most universal; black can look better on dark imagery
  • After converting, inspect the edges — semi-transparent edges are where problems show
